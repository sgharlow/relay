## Working title

**The one account that unlocks the other six**

Alternatives: *"It's never the bank that's the problem"* · *"The password that isn't on the list"*

---

## Draft

When my friend's mother went into hospital, the family did the sensible thing. They found her
handwritten list — the good kind, the one taped inside a kitchen cupboard, in careful capitals — and
they started working through it. Bank. Pharmacy. The electricity account. The insurance portal.

They got into none of them.

The list was accurate. Every password on it was correct, and every one of them was two years out of
date, because each of those companies had prompted her to change it at some point and she had done
what everyone does: changed it, moved on, and not gone back to the cupboard with a pen.

So they did what anyone does when a password does not work. They clicked *forgot password*. And that
is where it stopped, because every one of those reset links went to her email account — and her
email password was on the list too, and also wrong.

One account. Six locked doors behind it.

### It is almost never the account you are worried about

Families preparing for this tend to worry about the bank, because the bank is where the money is.
But banks are not usually the obstacle. Nearly every online account in an adult's life is designed
to be recoverable by whoever can read their email. That is the whole architecture of *forgot
password*. It is a convenience most of the time and a single point of failure exactly once.

Which means the real question is not *do we have the passwords*. It is *can someone reach the one
account the others recover through* — and for most families the honest answer is no, because nobody
has ever thought to ask it separately.

### The exercise, which takes twenty minutes and costs nothing

This is worth doing whether or not you ever buy anything to help you do it. Sit down with a sheet of
paper, or a spreadsheet if you prefer.

**One.** List the accounts that would actually matter in a crisis. Not everything — the ones where
being locked out for three weeks would cause a real problem. For most people that is somewhere
between five and a dozen: bank, utilities, phone, pharmacy or insurer, a pension or benefits portal,
maybe a landlord or mortgage account.

**Two.** Beside each one, write down which email address its password reset goes to. Not the email
they use most — the one *that account* has on file. These are often different, and the difference is
where families get caught. An account set up fifteen years ago may still be pointing at an address
nobody has opened since.

**Three.** Look at what you have written. In most households one address will appear beside nearly
every line. That is the master key, and it is usually the least-protected thing on the page.

**Four.** Now ask the awkward question about that one account. If the person who owns it could not
get to it for a month — in hospital, recovering, simply unable — could anyone else? Not *should*
they. *Could* they.

### What to do about the answer

If the answer is no, there are a few real options and they are not equally good.

The one most families reach for is to write the email password down and give it to someone. It works,
and it has a cost people tend to discover later: you cannot take it back. Once it is shared, it is
shared for good, with everyone that person tells and everyone who ever sees the note. It also tends
to be shared in a panic, over text, at the worst possible moment.

A better version is to use whatever the account already offers. Most major email providers now have
some form of emergency or legacy access built in — a trusted contact who can request entry after a
waiting period. It is free, it is already there, and almost nobody has turned it on. If you do only
one thing after reading this, do that one.

Beyond that there are password managers with emergency-access features, and there are dedicated
services for exactly this problem. Both are worth looking at. What matters more than the choice is
the property you are looking for: **can it be undone?** Anything you set up while everyone is well
should be reversible when everyone is well again, because the alternative is a system nobody sets up
until it is too late to set up calmly.

### The part people get wrong

The instinct is to treat this as paperwork — a list to be completed, filed, and forgotten. But a list
of passwords starts going stale the day it is written, for the same reason the one in the kitchen
cupboard did. Companies force changes. People upgrade phones. Two-factor codes move to a new device.

What does not go stale is **access to the account that recovers the others.** If one person other
than the account holder can get into that email in a genuine emergency, most of the rest of the list
becomes solvable in an afternoon. If nobody can, then the most carefully maintained list in the world
buys you very little.

That is the thing worth sorting out, and it is worth sorting out on an ordinary Tuesday, while it is
a twenty-minute admin task rather than the worst week of someone's life.
